How to budget for Christmas gifts

Updated September 2026

The method in four lines. Decide the total you can afford without borrowing. Divide it across the people you buy for, unevenly and on purpose. Write the number next to each name before you shop, not after. Then track what you spend against it as you go, because a budget you never look at again is just a wish.

Start from what you can afford, not from what gifts cost

Most Christmas overspending starts with the list rather than the money. You think about each person, decide what would be nice, add it up at the end, and discover the total. By then every number feels committed, because each one was chosen for a specific person you love, and cutting it feels like cutting them.

Doing it the other way round is uncomfortable for about ten minutes and then much easier. One number first. Everything else fits inside it.

A useful test for that number: if it went on a card, could you clear it by February without touching anything else you need? If not, it is not your budget, it is next year's problem wearing a bow.

Split it unevenly, and write it down

Equal splits feel fair and almost never survive contact with reality. You buy differently for a partner than for a colleague, and pretending otherwise just means the plan breaks in week two.

So put a real number next to each name. It can be lopsided. What matters is that the number exists before you start looking, because "about twenty or thirty" becomes forty-five in a shop, every time, and five of those is your whole overspend.

Count the costs that are not gifts

This is where budgets quietly fail. The gifts come in on target and the season still costs a third more than planned, because of everything around them.

  • Wrapping paper, tape, tags and cards
  • Postage, which is brutal if family lives abroad
  • Secret Santa at work, and the one at the club you forgot about
  • Teachers, neighbours, the person who looks after your cat
  • Food and drink you bring to other people's houses

Either give these their own line in the budget or accept that your gift number is really your gift number plus fifteen percent. Both are fine. Pretending they do not exist is not.

Track it while it is happening, not afterwards

A budget only does work if you look at it between deciding and paying. That is the whole mechanism. The moment you want is standing in a shop, holding something, knowing you have forty left for this person rather than a vague sense that things are probably fine.

Which means the budget has to live somewhere you can open one handed in eight seconds. A spreadsheet on a laptop at home fails this test, not because it is a bad tool but because it is in the wrong place at the only moment that matters.

The number nobody wants to look at

What you spent last year, per person.

If you have it, start there instead of from a blank page, because your real spending habits are better information than your intentions. If you do not have it, this year is the year to record it, and next December gets considerably easier. It also settles arguments with yourself about whether you are the kind of person who overspends at Christmas. The sheet knows.

What about starting earlier?

It genuinely works, and not for the reason people usually say. Buying in September does not mainly save money through sales. It saves money because you are not deciding under time pressure, and almost all gift overspending is panic converted into cash.

The catch is that buying early only helps if you write down what you bought. Otherwise you reach December, cannot remember what is in the cupboard, and buy a second present for the same person. A list you keep across the whole autumn is what makes early shopping actually cheaper.
